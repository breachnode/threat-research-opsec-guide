# Public Resources

Legitimate, public sources for threat-intelligence research. All of these are free to read and legal to cite. This list is a starting point, not an endorsement of any single vendor or viewpoint — cross-check important claims across at least two independent sources (see `methodology/03-source-hierarchy.md`).

## Government and law-enforcement

- **CISA (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency)** — cisa.gov — Advisories, alerts, and Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. The baseline citation for most defensive findings.
- **FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3)** — ic3.gov — Annual Internet Crime Reports and public service announcements on fraud trends. Also the place to report fraud-related material.
- **FBI / U.S. Secret Service public advisories** — Published alerts on financial fraud, business email compromise, and related schemes.
- **Europol / EC3 public reports** — Annual Internet Organised Crime Threat Assessment (IOCTA) and public advisories.
- **NIST National Vulnerability Database (NVD)** — nvd.nist.gov — Vulnerability data with severity scoring (CVSS) and references.

## Frameworks and standards

- **MITRE ATT&CK** — attack.mitre.org — The standard taxonomy of adversary tactics and techniques. Use it to map every finding to a technique ID where possible.
- **MITRE D3FEND** — d3fend.mitre.org — The defensive counterpart to ATT&CK: countermeasures mapped to techniques.
- **OWASP** — owasp.org — Web application security guidance, including the OWASP Top 10 and the Credential Stuffing Prevention Cheat Sheet.
- **NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)** — nist.gov/cyberframework — The standard framework for organizing defensive controls.

## Vendor threat-intelligence research (public blogs and reports)

Major security vendors publish free threat research drawn from their telemetry. Read them critically — note each vendor's data source and commercial incentives — and corroborate across vendors:

- **Vendor annual threat reports** (published by the major endpoint, network, and cloud security vendors) — useful for trends and base rates.
- **Vendor campaign write-ups** — detailed technical analysis of specific campaigns, usually with indicators and mitigations.
- **Cloud provider security blogs** — incident analyses and defensive guidance from the large cloud platforms.

## Academic and research sources

- **University and institute publications** on fraud, authentication, and underground-economy measurement — search via Google Scholar for peer-reviewed measurement studies.
- **USENIX Security, IEEE S&P, ACM CCS proceedings** — the major peer-reviewed security conferences; papers are typically freely available from the authors or the conference site.

## Security journalism and community analysis

- **Established security news outlets** (e.g., Krebs on Security, BleepingComputer, The Record) — timely coverage, often with primary-source reporting. Read the article, not the headline, and check whether claims are sourced.
- **SANS Internet Storm Center** — isc.sans.edu — Daily handler diaries on observed threats, written by practitioners.
- **CISA's Automated Indicator Sharing (AIS)** — for defenders who want machine-readable indicators from government sources.

## Reporting fraud and abuse

- **FBI IC3** — ic3.gov — Report internet crime, including fraud and identity theft.
- **FTC ReportFraud** — reportfraud.ftc.gov — Report fraud, scams, and identity theft.
- **Your bank or payment processor's fraud team** — for fraud affecting your own accounts or business.
- **The affected organization's abuse/security contact** — for phishing or fraud impersonating a specific company.

## A note on what is NOT listed here

This list deliberately contains no illicit marketplaces, forums, or channels, no leaked datasets, and no anonymous sources without provenance. If a research question cannot be answered from the sources above, the question is too broad — narrow it. The absence of a source type from this list is intentional.
